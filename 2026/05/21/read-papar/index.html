How to Read Papers

Created2026-05-21|Updated2026-05-21
|Post Views:

“Reading maketh a full man, conference a ready man, and writing an exact man.” — Francis Bacon

Staying current with AI research depends on how you read papers. I treat this post as a living note and will keep refining the workflow below.

1. Finding a Research Topic

Use LLMs to surface popular recent surveys in your area. Check whether a survey’s Discussion section points out limitations—those gaps often suggest research directions. On Google Scholar, papers that cite a survey are usually among the newest work in the field.

2. Rough Reading

A rough read is no longer “skim abstract and conclusion only.” I delegate this step to LLMs: ask for a summary plus an analysis of innovations and weaknesses. That helps most when the paper does not state limitations clearly.

3. Detailed Reading

Detailed reading means at least two passes—one fast, one slow—and two situations: familiar with the field or unfamiliar with the field.

3.1 Preface

For algorithm-heavy AI work today, if code is not open-sourced, I usually skip a deep read. Unstable datasets and unreproducible pipelines mean a method may look strong on paper but fail in practice. Fabricated results are, unfortunately, not rare. I do not support closed-source publication unless funding rules require confidentiality.

3.2 Familiar with the Field

  • Use an LLM on the Introduction to extract the problem and contributions.
  • Skip Related Work and go straight to Methods.

3.3 Unfamiliar with the Field

  • Read Related Work and identify the closest prior paper—authors usually explain it in more detail. You may need a mini deep-read of that paper too.

3.4 First Pass

  • Focus on main experiments only; ignore secondary ablations for now.
  • Avoid heavy note apps (Typora, Obsidian, etc.) in this pass. Prefer Zotero, EndNote, or similar tools for short inline annotations.

3.5 Second Pass

  • Read the method and open-source code carefully.
  • Be able to explain each step in your own words, especially the overview figure.
  • Read all experiments and ask why each one was designed.
  • If this paper is a strong baseline for your own work:
    • Rough-read the compared methods’ experimental setups.
    • Deep-read this paper’s setup line by line.

3.6 After the Second Pass

Consider a reading note on your blog. A useful template includes:

  • Problem statement
  • List of innovations
  • Full algorithm flow
  • Key experiments and data analysis
  • At least one self-drawn figure (besides figures from the paper)
  • Annotated code snippets where they matter

Before publishing, discuss and proofread the draft with an LLM.

3.7 Final Step

After the second pass, add a knowledge-graph link to the PDF so the reading loop is closed.

This is my personal workflow. It is still incomplete—I will keep updating it as I learn.
